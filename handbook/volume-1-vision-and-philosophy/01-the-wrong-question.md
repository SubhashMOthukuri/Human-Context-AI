# Chapter 1 — The Wrong Question

Consider two boys, born in the same city in the same year. They grow up
five streets apart. They attend the same school, sit through the same
curriculum, taught by the same teachers, in the same overcrowded classrooms
that smelled of the same chalk dust. By the measures anyone bothered to
take at the time — grades, teachers' remarks, the vague village consensus
about which children were "bright" — there was little to separate them.

We won't name them. Not because their names are a secret, but because
naming them would be the wrong move at the wrong time. The moment a name
enters the story, the mind reaches for a biography — dates, achievements, a
tidy arc from birth to significance. That reach is exactly the habit this
book is trying to break. So: call them nothing. Watch only what happens to
them.

Thirty years pass. One of them stands on a stage in Stockholm, or founds
the company that will outlive him, or writes the book that a hundred
million people will read. The other is still in the same city, doing work
he never wanted, having long since stopped mentioning the ambitions he had
at nineteen. Somewhere in the space between the same classroom and these
two endings, something happened. Not one thing — thousands of small
things. A teacher who noticed one of them and not the other. A book that
reached one of them at the right age and the other not at all. An uncle
with a business that needed an extra pair of hands one particular summer.
An illness. A scholarship that existed for one more year than it needed to.
A war that conscripted one and exempted the other. None of these, alone,
explains the divergence. All of them, together, are the divergence.

This is the part almost every account of a remarkable life skips. We are
extremely good, as a species, at describing what someone became. We are
almost entirely without practice at explaining why they became it instead
of something else. Ask how Stockholm happened and you will get an
achievement — a discovery, a body of work, a moment of recognition. Ask why
it happened to this person, in this place, at this time, and not to the boy
five streets over, and the confident tone disappears. That question
belongs to historians, and even they usually only attempt it once someone
has already become famous enough to justify the effort — which means the
method used to answer "why" is itself selected by the answer to "who."
Nobody commissions a biography of the boy who didn't win the prize.

This book exists because that asymmetry is a mistake, and because for the
first time it's a fixable one.

## What every existing answer is actually for

It's worth being precise about this, because the claim is not that nothing
today addresses human lives. A great deal does. The claim is that
everything available today was built to answer a different question than
the one this book is asking, and mistaking one for the other is the
central error to avoid.

A biography answers *what happened*. It is, at its best, a well-sourced
chronology with interpretation layered on top — this event, then this one,
then this one, connected by a narrator's judgment about which threads
matter. That judgment is valuable, but it is fixed at the moment of
publication, produced once by one author for one general reader, and
almost never shows its work: you receive the historian's conclusion about
why Lincoln did what he did, rarely the competing hypotheses and the
evidence weighed against each other in the open.

A search engine answers *where to find information*. It is extraordinarily
good at this and was never asked to be more. Type a person's name and you
receive a ranked list of documents that mention them — a doorway to
sources, not a synthesis of what those sources, read together, would tell
you about causation.

A large language model, asked about a person, answers *what can be
generated from what has been written about them*. This is a more
sophisticated act than it sounds — it involves genuine pattern-matching
across an enormous span of text — but it is fundamentally an act of
recombination, not investigation. It does not go and check a primary
source it hasn't seen. It does not tell you which parts of its answer rest
on a diary entry and which parts rest on inference. Ask it "why," and it
will produce a fluent paragraph shaped like an answer, with no way for you
to tell whether the paragraph is reporting something a historian
established or something the model is confabulating to fill the shape of
the question. Confidence and accuracy are, in these systems, two entirely
unrelated quantities.

A memory app, a genealogy service, a family archive answers *what should
be preserved*. It is a vessel, and a valuable one — photos, letters,
recordings, a family tree with names and dates. But a vessel does not
interpret its own contents. A box of letters is not the same thing as an
explanation of why the person who wrote them made the choices they made.

Notice the pattern. *What happened.* *Where to find it.* *What can be
generated.* *What should be preserved.* Four different, legitimate, useful
questions — and not one of them is *why did this person become who they
became.* That question has a specific shape: it requires holding a life
and its surrounding world in the same frame at the same time, tracing
influence rather than just sequence, and being honest about which
conclusions are supported and which are guesses. Nothing on the list above
was built to do that, which is not a criticism of any of them. A hammer
is not a bad screwdriver. It was never a screwdriver at all.

## Why "why" has been avoided

It isn't that no one has wanted to ask why. It's that answering well is
expensive, slow, and uncertain in a way that resists productization. Doing
it properly has historically required a trained historian, years of
archival work, fluency in the politics and economics and technology of a
period, and the discipline to say "the evidence doesn't settle this" when
it doesn't. That combination is rare and costly enough that it has only
ever been aimed at a handful of people — the ones already important enough
to justify a biographer's decade. Everyone else's "why" simply goes
unasked, then unanswerable, then — once the people who held the context in
their own memories have died — permanently unrecoverable.

There is a second reason "why" gets avoided, and it's more uncomfortable:
answering it honestly means tolerating ambiguity in a culture that prefers
verdicts. "Why did she succeed" has an answer people want — talent, grit,
vision, whatever the era's favored virtue happens to be — and that answer
is usually a story reverse-engineered from the outcome rather than a
conclusion reached by examining the person's life forward, from birth,
without knowing the ending in advance. A rigorous account of causation in
a human life should sometimes conclude "we don't know," or "several factors
plausibly contributed and we cannot rank them," or "this looks
overdetermined — remove any one cause and the outcome probably still
happens." Those are unsatisfying sentences. They are also, often, the true
ones. Any system that only ever hands back confident single-cause
narratives has optimized for satisfying the reader over informing them.

## What changed

Three things had to be true simultaneously for this to become possible, and
none of them were true even a decade ago. First, enough of the primary and
secondary record — letters, newspapers, court records, oral histories,
photographs, institutional archives — had to exist in digitized, searchable
form that a system could actually reach the evidence rather than only the
summaries of it. Second, language models had to become capable of holding
a large, heterogeneous body of material in view at once and reasoning
across it, rather than only retrieving the single most similar passage.
Third, and least discussed, the tooling to keep that reasoning honest —
to force every claim to point back at what supports it, and to represent
uncertainty as a first-class output instead of an embarrassing edge case —
had to mature enough to be buildable at all. Take away any one of the
three and this book describes a nice idea with no way to build it. With
all three in place, it describes a system.

None of this makes the system a historian, and it shouldn't try to be one.
It makes it something historians never had: a way to hold environment,
relationships, timing, culture, technology, and failure in the same frame
as a person's decisions, at a scale no single scholar could sustain across
more than a handful of lives in a career — and to do it while showing which
parts are documented and which parts are inference, every time.

## The claim

Strip away everything above and one sentence is left standing under it:

**A person cannot be understood by listing what they did — only by
tracing what shaped the fact that they did it, and being honest about how
well that tracing can be supported.**

Everything in this book is downstream of that sentence. The next chapter
takes the two unnamed boys from the opening and asks what it would actually
mean, structurally, to trace the shaping instead of the doing — and arrives
at an answer that looks less like a timeline and more like a graph, with
branches at every point where their two lives could still have gone either
way.
