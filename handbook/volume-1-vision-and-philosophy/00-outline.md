# Volume 1 — Vision & Philosophy

Target: 45–55 pages, 7 chapters, ~7–8 pages each. No technology in this
volume — that starts in Volume 2. This volume argues the *idea*; nothing
here should require the reader to trust an architecture diagram.

Flow: problem → consequence → philosophy → thesis.

## Chapter 1 — The Wrong Question
Opens on a story, not an argument: two children, same city, same year, same
school, five streets apart — one becomes a Nobel laureate, the other spends
his life in work he never wanted. Neither outcome was inevitable. We
remember who people became; we rarely ask why. From there, names what every
existing product actually optimizes for — biography answers "what
happened," chatbots answer "what can be generated," memory apps answer
"what should be preserved," search answers "where to find it." None of them
ask "why." Ends by stating the book's central claim in one sentence.

## Chapter 2 — Branching Lives
Formalizes the intuition from Chapter 1: a life isn't a fixed trajectory,
it's a branching graph, where every decision, relationship, or opportunity
opens a different set of possible futures. "Branch points," not
achievements, become the unit of analysis. This is the intuitive seed of
the knowledge graph that Volume 2/3 formalize.

## Chapter 3 — The Multiplier, Not the Ingredient
Argues environment, timing, and access multiply latent ability rather than
sit beside it. Illustrated with ordinary people, not legends: two software
engineers, same university, same GPA, same measured intelligence — ten
years later, one builds a startup, the other quits programming. The claim
has to hold for ordinary lives first; historical figures return in later
volumes as richer, evidence-backed case studies, not as this chapter's
proof. This is the chapter that justifies the Environment Engine before the
reader ever hears the term.

## Chapter 4 — Facts Are Not Understanding
A constructive reframing, not a takedown: Wikipedia answers "what
happened," Google answers "where to find information," an LLM answers "what
can be generated from information" — none of them answer "what combination
of context caused this outcome." That's not a shortcoming of those tools;
it's a different problem nothing has been built to answer. Positions
context as a missing computational primitive.

## Chapter 5 — The Cost of Not Understanding
The emotional case, not the intellectual one — grounded in the founder's
own grandfather and great-grandfather (see `../founding-problem.md`), told
first-person, scoped to that one lineage rather than the wider family.
Every branch of a family tree inheriting less of who came before as living
memory dies out; the requirement that this be reversible not for a
lifetime but across generations not yet born. Moves the reader from "this
is interesting" to "this matters," and widens the frame from historical
figures toward families — the actual motivation behind Phase 3/4 of the
roadmap, not just an extension of it.

## Chapter 6 — Understanding Over Prediction
States the epistemic stance the whole system inherits: hypotheses instead
of verdicts, uncertainty instead of false confidence, evidence attached to
every claim. Introduces the **Evidence Hierarchy** — Level 1: direct
evidence (letters, interviews, journals, footage); Level 2: historical
record; Level 3: contemporary secondhand observation; Level 4: AI
inference. Every explanation the system ever produces cites which level
it's standing on. Explains why claiming to know what someone "really"
thought is a category error — and why that restraint is a product
advantage, not a limitation.

## Chapter 7 — The Founding Thesis
Synthesis. Ends on one memorable, falsifiable sentence — not a diagram —
and states what would prove it wrong. Hands off to Volume 2, where the
causal-forces spine (Genetics → Family → Education → Environment →
Technology → Economy → Politics → Culture → Experience → Life) becomes a
formal model, one chapter per force. **Open question:** Chapter 1's thesis
covers *why* understanding matters; it may need a second half here covering
*for how long and for whom* — i.e., that the understanding has to persist
across every branch of a family tree, for descendants not yet born, for as
long as the system exists to carry it. Lightweight claim here; the actual
durability practices (formats, backups, no silent deletion) are Volume 5's
job, not this chapter's.

## Carried forward to Volume 2 (not lost — just not this volume's job)

- **History as a laboratory.** Historical figures aren't the end goal;
  they're the safest place to validate the system before it ever touches a
  living person's data. Volume 2 gets a dedicated section on why the first
  working system is built and judged against history.
- **Technology as first-class context.** Not an example nested under
  Environment — its own node in the causal-forces spine. What tools,
  discoveries, and constraints existed is a distinct question from what the
  economy or culture looked like, and deserves its own formal treatment.
