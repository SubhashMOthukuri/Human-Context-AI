# The Founding Problem

Kept here unpolished, in plain language, because the polished version
(Volume 1, Chapter 5, and wherever the longevity argument ends up) should
never be allowed to drift from why this exists.

## The loss

A grandfather's generation — a great-grandfather's generation — is already
being forgotten by the people alive today. Nobody captured who they really
were: not just what they did, but their character, their culture, what
they carried and would have wanted carried forward. Once the people who
held that in living memory are gone, it doesn't degrade slowly. It's just
gone, for every branch of the family descending from them, permanently.

## The requirement this creates

The system has to reverse this for descendants across every branch of a
family tree, including people not yet born. The "10,000 to 20,000 years"
framing is **not a literal engineering target** — it's not asking for
seed-vault-style institutional permanence or a legal-trust succession plan.
It's simpler and should be read that way: the system should keep telling
people's stories — where they were born, what happened to them, what they
carried — for as long as the application itself stays alive. The
obligation is to build for the maximum feasible longevity of an ongoing,
living product, not to design as if the company is expected to not exist.
Ordinary durability practices (backups, non-proprietary data formats,
not silently deleting anything) serve this; a permanent institution does
not need to be invented to satisfy it.

## The non-negotiable

It has to do this only with what's actually true. Store facts about who
these people were, not "improper answers" — confident-sounding
fabrication standing in for the real thing. In every other application of
this system, getting the "why" wrong is a quality problem. Here, it's
categorically worse: it's the second death of a memory the system exists
to prevent. This is the sharpest possible version of the Evidence
Hierarchy argument already planned for Chapter 6 — here it isn't an
epistemic nicety, it's the entire point.

## Data belongs to one family, not a directory

A user's access is scoped to their own family only. Someone using this to
preserve their grandfather's story cannot browse into, or edit, another
family's records. This isn't an incidental permission setting — it follows
directly from what the data is: someone's actual ancestor, not a public
figure or a shared resource. Every later volume that touches architecture
or product (access control, sharing, multi-user editing within one
family) has to treat family-level isolation as a hard boundary, not a
configurable default.

## How this relates to the strategic starting point

Volume 2's "history as a laboratory" idea — starting with historical
public figures rather than personal data — is the *strategy*. This is the
*motivation*. They aren't the same thing, and the book should say so
plainly: history is the safe rehearsal, on subjects with abundant
documentation and no consent issues, for a system that is ultimately being
built to be trusted with someone's own family — someone's own grandfather.

## Resolved (2026-08-01)

- **Chapter 5** grounds in the founder's own grandfather/great-grandfather,
  first-person, real — not an invented composite — but scoped to that one
  lineage only, not a sprawling account of the wider family. No requirement
  to include real names; "my grandfather" is enough.
- **Longevity** is the "keep the application alive and telling these
  stories" reading above, not literal 10,000-year institutional
  engineering. Volume 5's parking-lot note has been softened to match.
